How the 'Sephora Kids' Trend Became a Costly Business Problem

Virality isn't always free publicity—sometimes it's a financial and reputational liability.

By Ava Nordlicht · January 26, 2026 · Pop Culture

What began as a viral social-media joke has evolved into a real financial and strategic challenge for the beauty industry. The rise of so called “Sephora kids” - preteens crowding luxury beauty stores, purchasing high-end skincare, and filming content - has exposed weaknesses in how modern beauty brands market, price, and protect their products. At the center of the phenomenon is Sephora, a retailer designed for adult consumers with disposable income. In recent months, videos showing children testing expensive products, damaging displays, and overwhelming stores have gone viral, sparking backlash from customers and dermatologists alike. While the attention initially appeared beneficial, the trend has quietly cost retailers and brands significant money.

The root of the issue lies in influencer-driven marketing. Over the past decade, beauty companies shifted away from traditional advertising toward social-media platforms like TikTok, where visually appealing products can reach millions overnight. Skincare brands emphasized bright packaging, simple routines, and emotional language such as “self-care” and “must-have,” unintentionally appealing to younger audiences. For Sephora, the financial consequences have been tangible. Stores have reported increased losses from damaged testers and unsellable inventory, particularly among premium skincare brands with high replacement costs. Unlike makeup testers, skincare products are often contaminated once opened, forcing stores to discard entire units. These losses add up quickly across hundreds of locations. Equally costly is the impact on Sephora’s core customer base. Adult shoppers - who generate the majority of revenue - have expressed frustration with overcrowded stores, limited access to products, and a degraded shopping experience. As a result, some high-value customers have reduced in store shopping or shifted purchases online, weakening Sephora’s most profitable channel.

There are also long-term brand risks. Dermatologists have warned that children using strong active ingredients such as retinol and exfoliating acids may damage their skin, raising concerns about safety and liability. Once a product designed for adults becomes associated with harm to minors, reputational damage can extend far beyond a single viral trend. The “Sephora kids” phenomenon highlights a broader lesson for the beauty industry: marketing strategies optimized solely for attention can erode control over a brand’s audience. By prioritizing virality without clear consumer boundaries, companies blurred the line between aspirational and inappropriate use. In response, Sephora has begun adjusting in-store policies, including limiting tester access and increasing staff oversight. However, the larger correction may come from brands themselves, through more responsible influencer partnerships, clearer product positioning, and packaging that reflects function rather than hype. What started as a social-media joke has become a case study in how modern retail can lose money by winning attention. In the beauty industry, visibility alone is no longer enough - control, clarity, and customer trust matter just as much.
