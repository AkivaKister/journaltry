Why We Still Believe in Gold

The Psychology Behind America’s Oldest Store of Wealth

Amalia Chalamish · April 23, 2026 · Markets & Finance

Even in an age of digital currency and Bitcoin, gold still has a powerful grip on the American mindset. In addition to the California Gold Rush, which established gold as one of the first propellers of national economic expansion, several psychological aspects contribute to why Americans view gold as a “safe haven”.

The United States’ currency was directly linked to a fixed quantity of gold, called the Gold Standard, which was instituted from 1879 to 1933. The Gold Standard was suspended by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in response to people hoarding gold during the Great Depression. People would rush to exchange their paper money for gold as quickly as they could, one of the best displays of Americans’ trust in the shiny metal during uncertain times.

A safe haven is an asset that is expected to increase in value during times of market volatility, economic downturns, or geopolitical turmoil. In recent months, gold has reached its highest price ever, $5,589.38 per ounce on January 28, 2026. The record-high directly overlapped with escalating conflict between the U.S. and Iran, aggressive tariff policies, and record levels of gold purchasing by central banks. Just around five years earlier, in August 2020, gold reached the $2,000 threshold for the first time during the uncertainty of the COVID-19 pandemic and rising U.S.-China tensions. Gold reached another all-time high in 2011 in response to the global debt crisis.

In the past year alone, Gold has outpaced the Swiss Franc, Japanese Yen, and U.S. bonds. It is clear that in times of uncertainty, gold prices have surged to all-time highs, but it begs the question again and again: why gold?

To fully understand gold’s enduring role in the American mindset, one must first examine a broader belief system that has long shaped the nation's economic and cultural identity: The American Dream.

The American Dream, the belief that any American can achieve financial prosperity and a better life regardless of their background, is rooted in an idea deeper than economic success alone. At its core, it depends on trust in a stable and fair system and faith that opportunity exists for those willing to seize it.

The same psychological foundation helps explain the lasting appeal of gold. Like the American Dream, gold derives its power not only from its material value but from a collective belief in its reliability.

In 1848, hundreds of thousands of Americans and immigrants rushed west in pursuit of sudden wealth, an event named the California Gold Rush. Few had a guarantee of success, and many ultimately failed, yet the movement was driven by a powerful collective belief: that gold represents a real and attainable opportunity. This ideology was ingrained in the American psyche years before it had a name. Only later would James Truslow Adams define it as the American Dream.

The connection is so deeply embedded that it persists in everyday language. The phrase “a gold mine”, defined as an opportunity to generate significant wealth, reveals how gold has come to symbolize more than material value; it represents the very concept of opportunity. In this way, gold mirrors the psychological foundation of the American Dream: a trusted pathway to advancement.

Psychological research indicates that trust is not purely rational, but is deeply tied to what feels certain and tangible. Studies show that individuals are more likely to trust what they can physically perceive, as tangible objects reduce uncertainty and provide a sense of stability. Unlike abstract systems such as digital currencies, gold offers something visible, finite, and enduring. This tangibility makes it psychologically reassuring, reinforcing the perception that it is a reliable source of value, even in times of uncertainty.

Gold’s appeal is also deeply rooted in human psychology, specifically in response to its color and physical properties. The metal’s reflective, glowing surface naturally draws human attention, triggering emotional responses tied to warmth, strength, and even survival. Across cultures, the luminous quality has led gold to be linked with divinity, power, and success, from ancient Egypt to modern-day awards like Olympic medals.

The psychological attraction strengthens gold as trustworthy. Since Humans are instinctively drawn to what is visually striking, gold’s appearance does more than signal wealth, it reinforces it. In this way, gold’s value is not only constructed by markets or history, but also through deeply embedded cognitive and emotional responses that make it feel both desirable and dependable.

Gold’s enduring appeal reveals something deeper about the American mindset itself. Americans have long been driven by a belief in stability paired with the pursuit of opportunity, a desire not just to succeed but to trust that success is possible. Gold embodies both. In this sense, Americans do not just invest in gold; they believe in it, the same way they believe in the idea that opportunity is always within reach.
