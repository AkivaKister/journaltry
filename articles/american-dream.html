The Myth of the American Dream

The American Dreams new address, in Denmark

Tova Samansky · June 7, 2026 · Opinions

When the history of a community is written, the ink is often reserved for those standing at the podium or those whose names appear at the top of a press release. The story is usually told through people most visible at defining moments. Beneath that version of events, however, exists another record: one carried by hidden heroes whose presence shaped the community in quieter but equally permanent ways.

The morning of March 12th, 2026. Teachers arrived at their classroom ready and prepared to take on the day’s obstacles with a smile stretched from ear to ear. Security guards woke up, like any other morning, ready to protect their community. However, unlike the usual routines these people were used to, this day brought up some different sorts of challenges.

We hear the story of March 12th through the voices of leaders like Rabbi Jen Lader, but if we look closer at the architecture of that day, we find that the structure was held up by a different group of people. The true "hidden heroes" of the March 12th incident were the teachers. The individuals who, without a moment’s notice, transformed their classrooms from spaces of academic learning into sanctuaries of absolute protection. To understand their role is to understand the very essence of the title Moshe Rabbeinu, or "Moses our Teacher."

In the Jewish tradition, we do not refer to Moses as "Moses the King" or "Moses the Lawgiver," despite his immense power. We call him Moshe Rabbeinu. This choice of words is intentional and suggests that the highest form of leadership is not found in commanding an army, but in the patient, often invisible work of guiding a soul, or simply the act of teaching. On the tragic morning of March 12th, the teachers in the Michigan community lived up to this ancient standard. When the atmosphere shifted and the threat became real, these educators didn't have the luxury of panic. Like Moshe standing before the burning bush, hesitant, perhaps, but ultimately saying "Hineni" (Here I am), they stepped into a role they never asked for but were uniquely prepared to fill.

The job of a teacher is a strange and beautiful paradox. On a normal day, it requires the patience to explain a concept for the tenth time, the empathy to notice a student who is having a bad week, and the genuine desire to care for them. But as we saw on March 12th, that same job can suddenly demand the tactical precision of a first responder. The "hidden hero" doesn't wear a uniform; they wear the same cardigan or lanyard they wore the day before. Their heroism isn't found in a single loud act, but in the quiet, steady way they gathered their students, lowered their voices to keep the room calm, and placed their own bodies between the door and the children in their care.

This is the "Moshe" model of leadership: a leader who is defined by their service to the flock. Just as Moshe carried the burdens of the Israelites through the wilderness, these teachers carried the emotional weight of an entire community’s children during a moment of crisis. They managed the impossible task of maintaining order while their own hearts were undoubtedly racing. They became the "calm in the center of the storm," ensuring that for the students, the world still felt like a place where adults were in control and safety was a guarantee.

We often think of heroes as people who perform extraordinary deeds in the spotlight, gaining recognition from the whole community. However, the teachers of March 12th remind us that the most profound heroism is often found in the fulfillment of a sacred duty. They did not act for the sake of a headline; they acted because, to a teacher, a student is not just a stranger who they met by mere coincidence, but rather a sacred responsibility. They are the guardians of our future, working in the shadows of the classroom to ensure that the light of the next generation is never extinguished.

While we may have learned the details of that day through the reports of others, the heartbeat of the story belongs to the educators. They are the modern-day successors to Moshe Rabbeinu, proving that the greatest leaders are those who teach us not just how to read or write, but how to remain brave, how to stay kind, and how to protect one another when the world outside becomes uncertain.

Their names may not be on every plaque, but their impact is written on the safety and the resilience of every preschool student who walked out of those doors on March 12th.
