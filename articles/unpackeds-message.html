Know Your Story

Unpacked’s Message for the Youth of Tomorrow

Lihi Maine and Leah Barzideh · April 23, 2026 · Opinions

Picture a Jewish student in a college seminar, engaged in a heated debate on the Middle East conflict. Someone challenges her on the “colonialist origins” of Israel. She knows she disagrees. She conjures images of affluent European men stomping on the well-earned crops of indigenous people and recognizes the misalignment between that image and everything she knows to be true about her nation’s homeland. But when bombarded with accusations, buzzwords, and misinformation, she reaches for words that aren’t there—not because she doesn’t care, but because she never learned how to tell her story.

She is not alone.

In recent years, Jewish students around the world have found themselves navigating an increasingly uncomfortable reality. Antisemitism has become more visible—in high schools, on university campuses, and across social media. Conversations about Israel and Jewish identity have grown more charged, and many young people feel mounting pressure to represent their community in discussions that can turn hostile faster than expected. The problem, though, isn’t that Jewish students lack conviction. Most have plenty of it. The problem is that conviction, on its own, has a ceiling. When the conversation becomes complex—when someone cites Deir Yassin or the Balfour Declaration—caring deeply about something is not the same as being able to speak about it.

This is where much of Jewish education has quietly fallen short. And where Unpacked for Educators is trying to do something different.

Unpacked for Educators is a Jewish educational media platform that explains Israel, Jewish history, and current events in a way that does not ignore the complexity embedded in the Middle East conflict. Through videos, podcasts, articles, and classroom curricula, it gives students and educators tools to engage with difficult topics—not by simplifying them, but by understanding them from multiple angles. The goal is not to hand young Jews a script, but to help them grasp the full arc of the Jewish story—including its difficult chapters—so that when they walk into a room like that seminar, they are not scrambling. As Unpacked for Educators’ leadership has put it, they believe students can learn the “goosebumps of complexity”: celebrating Israel while honestly reckoning with its harder chapters.

A study by Boundless Israel found that among American Jews aged 18 to 40, those who described their Israel education as fair and balanced were 15 percentage points more likely to feel connected to Israel and to see it as central to their Jewish identity. That is not a small number. It suggests the instinct to protect students from complexity—to keep the narrative clean and uncomplicated—may actually do the opposite of what it intends. Connection is not undermined by complexity; it depends on it.

While most are caught up in the noise—the chanting, the shouting, the tweets written in all caps with five exclamation points—something quieter has been shifting in Jewish education. It does not photograph well. It is not packaged in slogans or optimized for thirty-second clips. It is, frankly, slow. And in a media environment that rewards speed above almost everything else, slow tends to go unnoticed.

At the center of that shift are people like Keren Horn, who brings this mission to life through Unpacked for Educators.

Mrs. Horn is a Jewish educator specializing in curriculum development in Israel and Holocaust education. She spent years as a classroom teacher, built a comprehensive multi-year Israel education program, and has presented on Israel and Zionism to educators, teens, and parents across the country. She holds a Master’s in Education in Learning, Cognition, and Development from Rutgers University. She is now a senior educator with Unpacked for Educators and program director of Unpacked Scholars, a new teen initiative. But none of that is what defines her work most. What defines it is a lesson she learned from Dr. Noam Weissman: a countercultural approach to engagement.

Before you post. Before you argue. Before you defend. Know. Your. Story.

Knowing your story means understanding the history behind the headline. It means being able to explain 1948 without hesitation—the triumphs and the tragedies, not just the version that is easiest to tell. It means acknowledging that Israel has made mistakes while still believing in its legitimacy, without treating those ideas as contradictions. And it means distinguishing between legitimate criticism and dehumanization—a distinction that becomes difficult under pressure if you do not know your own history.

There is a reason financial literacy educators emphasize emotional regulation. Emotional investing leads to bad decisions: panic selling, impulsive buying, and the cycle that follows. The remedy is not only more information, but understanding that holds steady under volatility. Jewish identity is not so different. Students who know their history deeply are less rattled by slogans—not because they have been shielded from hard questions, but because they have already sat with them.

The approach Mrs. Horn describes requires discomfort. It requires holding complexity without flattening it into simplicity. It resists the pull of the satisfying one-liner. The goal is not polished spokespeople, but students who understand the arc of Jewish history well enough to share it honestly—and to elevate the conversations they enter.

To understand why this approach is not only effective but necessary, one must recognize that this conflict is not only a war of facts—it is a war of perception and feeling. And historically, one of the most persistent tools used against the Jewish people has been dehumanization. The response is not only argument, but rehumanization. Rehumanization comes through telling a full story grounded in both knowledge and personal connection. Facts may persuade the mind, but narrative gives a conversation its emotional weight. A lived, honest story—shaped by both connection and complexity—creates something far more durable than a rehearsed argument.

Think back to that student in the seminar. Now imagine her a year later. Same room. Same challenge. This time, she does not search for words that are missing. She already has them. And that changes everything.
