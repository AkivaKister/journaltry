The Rise of Bitcoin and Its Peak

A decentralized digital currency that went from zero to $126,000—and changed how a generation thinks about money.

By Nathan Kohl · April 14, 2026 · Pop Culture

Bitcoin is a decentralized digital currency created in 2009 by the mysterious Satoshi Nakamoto, initially as an experiment but later gaining massive global value and attention. Unlike traditional money, it operates without banks or governments, using blockchain technology to securely verify transactions and limit supply to 21 million coins, which creates scarcity.

Over time, growing trust and demand led to major price increases, including a surge to nearly $20,000 in 2017 and a peak around $126,000 in 2025 after increased institutional investment. However, its price has also been volatile, with sharp rises followed by declines.

For many young people, Bitcoin was their first encounter with investing. It was accessible, exciting, and—for those who bought early—enormously profitable. It was also, for many who bought near peaks, a sobering introduction to market risk.

Bitcoin's rise also introduced millions of young people to concepts like blockchain, decentralized finance, and monetary policy. In discussing Bitcoin, students are implicitly discussing inflation, the role of central banks, the nature of currency, and the ethics of financial systems. That's a remarkable curriculum delivered through a single asset.

The deeper story of Bitcoin isn't really about price. It's about what happens when a generation distrustful of institutions builds its own financial infrastructure. Whether that infrastructure proves durable over decades remains to be seen. But the impulse behind it—to participate in financial systems on one's own terms—is both understandable and worth taking seriously.

Today, Bitcoin remains a highly influential but debated innovation, seen by some as the future of money and by others as a speculative bubble. Whatever its ultimate fate, it has already significantly changed how people think about finance and technology.
