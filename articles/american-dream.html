The Myth of the American Dream

The American Dreams new address, in Denmark

Tova Samansky · June 7, 2026 · Opinions

If a person was born into a low-income family in the United States, reaching just the middle class would take about five generations on average. In Denmark, however, it only takes on average two. The so-called “American Dream” is alive and well, it just happens to have a Denmark passport and live in Copenhagen.

In March 2019, more than 50 individuals were accused of or assisting in high schoolers having their way bought into elite universities: whether by forging sports records, cheating on tests, or simply paying off administrators. The scandal made headlines and shocked the public; however, Harvard philosopher, Michael Sandel in his book The Tyranny of Merit, argues that in an unequal society, such as America, those who are born on top must believe their success is earned and credited to their talent not bought. The best way to do that is by using elite colleges. By obtaining those credentials are just a means to an end; where only those who are privileged enough can make it to such an end. The scandal was not a departure from meritocracy. It was meritocracy with the mask pulled off.

The belief that economic outcomes reflect individual merit ignores the compounding advantages of inherited wealth, unequal educational access, and social networks — and that belief is most convenient for those who benefit from their social placing. At first glance, it is an appealing idea. The notion that hard work and talent determines where you end up in life, rather than your family name and area code deciding your future before you ever had a chance to object. This is the idea that most Americans would agree sounds fair. It is the foundation of the American Dream, the idea that one can rise from poverty through hard work, even marketed to sound possible. However, when taking a look at the statistics of it there is a hidden truth: this system regards who you know, not just what you can do.

Research by Harvard's Opportunity Insights lab grew interested in this idea. He found that nearly one third of Americans will work in the same firm as one of their parents. This means that children with wealthy parents are disproportionately likely to find work in a competitive job market. These children are given the opportunity to gain the experience that will propel them to even higher positions merely because of their family, not their skills. Also, within the job, they are likely to earn twenty percent more. Even after turning 30, the odds are still in privileged children's favor. Researcher Matthew Staiger found people are 200 times more likely to work for a parent's firm, no matter the age, than a different company in the same area, a gap that can not be explained with experiences or capability, but instead driven by the use of social connections.

There is a difference between merit as a value and this system. The first just means competence matters, you want a qualified surgeon operating on you, no matter where they grew up. The second is a bigger claim. That the economic order we grow up in reflects what people deserve. And this claim is where the American Dream falls short. Once you accept that the system is fair, the people at the bottom feel like they did something to end up there. Meritocracy does not just reward the winners, instead it blames the losers.

Meritocracy is an ideology that is convenient for those on the top. It is fundamentally a name for a system that allowed privilege to be passed off as talent. The system allows for wealthy children to buy their way into a life that allows them to maintain family wealth for generations to come. Believing in this system does not only promote the idea that wealth deserves to stay rich but it shifts blame onto those the system was never designed to serve.
