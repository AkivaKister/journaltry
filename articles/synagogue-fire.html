REKINDLING HOPE: Painting Survives Devastating Synagogue Fire

When a raging wildfire tore through her Pasadena synagogue, Rebecca Guberman feared everything she loved was lost.

The Advantage · February 23, 2026 · Community

AUTHOR’S NOTE

This piece is written in the spirit of historical inquiry and mortal reflection. It does not seek to indict Christianity, Catholicism, or the figure of Jesus, but rather examine how centuries of misinterpretation surrounding the crucifixion have fueled antisemitism. Jesus is portrayed here as a moral and spiritual teacher whose life and teachings deserve respect; the critique offered is directed at distorted narratives and their consequences, not at faith itself.

INTRODUCTION

Antisemitism feels like a vast dark ocean that, once I try to paddle my fragile body up to the surface for a gulp of air, sends another wave washing over me, suffocating me once more. When you are standing in the shallow end, antisemitism’s currents become slow and inconsistent, and it is easy to just stare at its hypnotizing pulsations before realizing they will gradually become so large and insufferable that even if you are in the most tranquil place, their waves sweep you off your feet and move the ground from under you. I write this piece not for its controversiality nor for the backlash I will inevitably receive. I write this for the one person who reads my writing and whose mind shifts tracks, changes course. Although this is a hard piece to write, the strongest person is one who not only accepts their vulnerabilities but broadcasts them out into the world for everyone to hear. So just as the principal Jewish prayer, the Shema, means listen, I beg of you to open your heart and allow my ideas to ruminate inside your head for even just a moment before you try to silence my voice.

Lately, it feels like I have gotten to that point, the point where even as sheltered as I am, I cannot ignore the tsunami that has washed away my walls and forced me to face my life’s largest battle headfirst. It all started when I was talking to one of my dearest friends about religion. I was talking about how often I visit my synagogue and what the space was comprised of. I described the ornate ark with vibrant colors that dazzled me. A tree of life stood in the middle, and on each of its sprawled bony branches lay the names of my ancestors: first Sarah and Abraham, then Rebekah and Isaac, finally Rachel and Jacob. You do not really understand, fundamentally, that people are different from you. We judge each other because our mindsets do not align. Simple as that. But despite that, the conquest for objective truth is eternal. She looked me in the eyes, her expression growing more lifeless with each word. “But where is Judah?” she asked, looking around the room uncomfortably. Ignorant, I replied, “Well, Judah Maccabee wasn’t a patriarch,” looking into her sclera. Her skin seemed to cinch as if there were an internal vacuum inside her body. “No! Judah the apostle?!” I did not know who he was. Jews in Sunday School only study the Old Testament, or as we call it, the Torah. This man Judah, whom I assumed at the time had the last name Apostle, had never once occurred in my mind. “The Jew that killed Christ?” Her eyes were now not only cruel but active weapons. I had known for a fact that it was the Romans who killed Christ, so I defensively countered, “No, the Romans killed Christ.” At that time my blood felt furiously hot, and I felt as if I were drowning in uncomfortable warmth. “Well, that is what not we believe in my religion. “She responded with vigor. My heart sank. What a traitor echoed in my head, and yet, ironically, she was calling me one. “We are still friends,” she said, coldly patting me on the shoulder. But my heart felt as cold as midnight stone. This story is what I had wished I had told her. This is what I had hoped she would open one ear and close the other to allow my words to truly sink in.

NOTE: The following is a reinterpretation of the crucifixion of Jesus Christ, and there is no explicit evidence in the New Testament that confirms or refutes Judas Iscariot’s internal reasoning or moral deliberation when he reported Jesus to the Roman authorities.

CRUCIFIXION

In a small village named Kerioth in southern Israel lived a young man who, like most people, believed that the Messiah had not come. His eyes were dull and dark, his clothes humble despite his immense wealth, and his beard scraggly and untamed. He thought deeply and constantly that the Messiah would come and rebuild the parched, crumbling, blood-shed Earth. He would be a magnificent mortal, a descendant of King David, a warrior fighting for the existence of universal knowledge of God and for justice to be restored to humanity. This young man was Judas Iscariot, Hebrew name

Judah, born into an aristocratic wealthy family. After his family disowned him for gravitating toward John the Baptist’s preachings, he set his sights on strengthening his Jewish maturity by becoming treasurer of the apostles. He met Jesus when he was 30 years of age. Although Jesus had supposedly touched the lives of hundreds and mended the hearts of many, he did not seem to be “the one” in Judas’s eyes. Judaea had become a Roman province in 6 AD, with limited autonomy allowed to Jewish authorities. Since then, Jews were forced to diminish their religious practices, bow down to Caesar, and deal with high taxes and a plethora of cultural tensions. Several revolts were desperately launched against the Romans, yet none succeeded. Once again, Jews were shackled, wrists behind their backs, doing back-breaking slave labor reminiscent of Egypt. While Judas loved Jesus’s compassion and Jewish-rooted ideals, he grew increasingly enraged at his acquiescence to repressive Roman rule. Jesus said he was focused on a spiritual mission, but would not a real Messiah stand up and liberate the Jews just as Moses did in Egypt? He even taught people to render unto Caesar, emphasizing a Kingdom of G*d not based in political power. Though Jesus’s modest upbringing kept him grounded, he did not see things behind the curtain as Judas had. Coming from a wealthy background, Judas had the unique vantage point of seeing how policy could be easily tarnished and corrupted by games of power. How could someone so brilliant and progressive overlook the fact that without religious freedom, one cannot live a happy, prosperous life? Do we remain silent? Do we risk not voicing our opinions before they silence ours forever? How could someone be so blind? Judas’s head filled with pounding anger and confusion. It felt like seeing a dove trapped in a cage, only to realize the dove had chosen not to escape the hidden opening. During the Passover Seder in Jerusalem, surrounded by carved peak-shaped pillars pressing inward, his doubts expanded and seemed to occupy the room itself. As the thirteenth disciple, Judas sat beside Jesus, watching his glowing eyes and widening smile. Little did he know it would be the last supper. Although Jesus was a giver and provider, Judas believed he ignored the covenant to seek and protect justice. Resentment brewed. To gaze upon captured Jews and believe this was the path of peace, was this what God promised? When Moses saw the Egyptian taskmaster whipping a Jewish slave, he acted. Why would Jesus not do the same? Judas did not doubt Jesus’s goodness or compassion. What tormented him was the fear that spiritual redemption alone could not shield his people from continued suffering under Roman rule.

This is where the story diverges. In many traditional interpretations, Judas’s motivations are portrayed as purely evil, greed for thirty pieces of silver, an extension of Satan’s control. While this belief fueled hostility toward Jews for generations, from a Jewish perspective the idea is more complex. Judas was disowned by his parents for his beliefs and believed deeply in Jesus’s mission. That is the mark of a believer. Disagreement with Jesus’s response to Roman power did not erase that devotion. Judas made an immoral decision not because of innate evil, but because of a feeling of betrayal, that someone so compassionate could witness oppression and remain politically silent.

Judas trembled before two Roman soldiers and kissed them on the cheek. The weight of this decision was suffocating, yet he believed he acted in pursuit of justice. A soldier extended thirty silver coins as a reward. He led them down a dusty winding road to a quiet garden filled with olive trees. Despite the abundance of olive branches, Judas offered none. He watched Jesus’s eyes fill with tears, his expression hardening into silence.

Here is an essential distinction: the Jews did not kill Christ. The Romans executed him and placed the inscription “King of the Jews” above the cross. This fact has often been overshadowed in discussions of antisemitism. The belief that Jews killed Christ persisted for centuries until October 28, 1965, when Nostra Aetate formally clarified that Jews were not collectively responsible. This misreading inflicted devastating harm on millions of Jews worldwide and still persists in some communities today. Judas’s actions do not represent all Jews, just as one Catholic’s actions do not represent all Catholics. Yet this simple truth often slips from public memory.

Once Judas kissed his friend’s cheek, the world seemed to drain of color. Roman soldiers seized Jesus and led him to the cross. He was speared, and the world fell silent. Judas collapsed under unbearable guilt and later took his own life.

CONCLUSION

Even as Jesus’s resurrection symbolizes redemption, the story of his death was weaponized into a narrative that fueled centuries of antisemitism. Christianity and Judaism share many foundations yet diverge in the question of what the Messiah must provide to society. Something in my heart tells me that the tide of antisemitism would begin to recede if people surrendered certainty, embraced humility, and listened with open minds and unburdened hearts.
